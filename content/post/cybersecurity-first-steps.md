## Cell Service and ISPs (Internet Service Providers)

You've almost certainly heard of VPNs from annoying ads for "Cyber Ghost" "Express VPN", or "Nord VPN" or whatever. In truth VPNs are often a huge privacy NEGATIVE because you forgo [4th Amendment protections and wiretapping laws](https://www.ludlowinstitute.org/articles/https/nbtvsubstackcom/p/is-the-government-running-a-dragnet) that are present for your American cellular data service or home internet service. What a VPN does is take all your internet traffic that originates from your phones IP address which is given to you by your ISP or cell company, and routes it all through a Third Party server so all of your traffic that websites and services see looks like it's coming from that Third Party server. The major issue here is that, your internet connection that is assigned by your ISP is unique to you, and because the IP address is linked to you, there's 100% certainty that anything coming from that IP address is you. That's pretty bad for being anonymous on the internet, but it also means that the CLOUD act and FISA act can't apply, because those laws require state intelligence to have reason to believe foreign nationals are using the service. Because VPN servers are used by Americans and foreign nationals alike, they have full legal precedence to log all the ins and outs of each server without a warrant, which includes YOUR internet traffic.

VPNs are still essential for true internet anonymity, but specifically only the most trusted ones like [Mullvad](https://mullvad.net/en) and [NYM](https://nym.com/) which allow you to route your traffic through two different countries at once, providing obfuscation as to where your traffic is coming from even if state agencies are logging the ins and outs.

* These VPNs will have you running into many website blocks, will be much slower in both bandwidth (download speed) and latency (how long it takes for web connections to initiate) so are unsuitable for daily usage IMO as you'll end up becoming frustrated and turning them off anyway as even I find myself doing.
* The gold standard are networks like the Tor network and i2p network which are both "Darknets" but this is so beyond the scope that I'd say don't even worry about it.
    
There are some problems here though. You have to reduce the legal loophole discussed before, the "Third Party Exception". Luckily in Virginia we have the recently passed [Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDA)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacodefull/title59.1/chapter53/) which makes it such that you can opt out of Third Party data sharing from your ISP and phone provider. Here are the links for [Verizon](https://www.verizon.com/privacy/your-data), [Spectrum](https://www.spectrum.com/policies/your-privacy-rights-opt-out) and [Cox](https://www.cox.com/aboutus/policies/online-privacy-policy.html), let me know if you have a different ISP at home. For your phone it varies more but [here's a good article](https://www.cape.co/blog/how-to-opt-out-of-data-sharing).

* This is important to do because as discussed previously, you retain your 4th Amendment rights for your cellphone location data and internet browsing data via cases like [Carpenter v United States](https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/supreme-court-case-library/carpenter-v-united-states) and laws like the VCDA IF AND ONLY IF you haven't "AGREED" for these Third Parties to sell/share your data.
* As examined previously, you can see the chain of logic for legal loophole here. Despite 4th amendment protections, a vast majority of people won't opt-out of the data sharing that they "agreed" to when they "agreed" to the terms of service that was 20+ pages long and was required to actually use the service. Still evil and needs to be changed, but luckily there's an out.

**TL;DR** don't use just a random VPN, if you want anonymity and true privacy, selectively use a trusted multihop VPN like Mullvad, iVPN or NYM in VPN mode, or better yet mixnet like Tor, i2p or NYM mixnet mode. For most of your traffic use your standard internet connection but ensure you have opted out of all data sharing to retain your God given, constitutionally protected, 4th amendment rights against unwarranted search and seizure. Refer to the DNS section below to improve your privacy and security of your internet connection.

## DNS

DNS or Domain Name Service is a core component of the internet. As the name suggests it deals with domain names, which are the .coms, .nets, .whatevers. For instance one of my domains is djunaedi.net which you can see in my email address [mitchell@djunaedi.net](mailto:mitchell@djunaedi.net). When you go to, say, my website at [mitchell.djunaedi.dev](https://mitchell.djunaedi.dev/) your computer asks your configured DNS server what IP address "mitchell.djunaedi.dev" is associated with. The DNS server then responds with "45.77.152.101" which is the actual computer (servers are just specialized computers) that is listening on that IP address for web traffic. Then your device initiates a HTTPS handshake creating a secure connection, then my server sends your computer the website info.

* You can verify this IP address by going to [mxtoolbox.com](https://mxtoolbox.com/DnsLookup.aspx) and typing in "mitchell.djunaedi.dev". I use it pretty often it's very useful for debugging and curiosity. You can see what company I use for my webserver: The Constant Company, LLC and what nameserver authoritatively provides the IP address associated with the domain which is ns1.desec.io. Any DNS server you use will ask ns1.desec.io what IP is associated with mitchell.djunaedi.dev.

This DNS name resolution is a one way process, however. If you only had the IP address and only saw that you were connected via an encrypted HTTPS connection to 45.77.152.101, then all that can be done is what's called a reverse DNS query. This will return whatever the ISP that serves the internet connection to 45.77.152.101 has configured when a computer asks it what domain is associated with it.

* For 45.77.152.101 the reverse DNS answer is "45.77.152.101.vultrusercontent.com". Not very useful, huh? You can verify this again at [mxtoolbox.com](https://mxtoolbox.com/SuperTool.aspx#), this time select "Reverse Lookup" and type in 45.77.152.101 and you'll get that answer from the nameserver "reversedns.vultr.com". Vultr is the server provider for The Constant Company, the ISP that came up from the inquiry before.

Putting these two points of information together we can now get a picture about how most internet service provider tracking is done. Since website connection content is encrypted via HTTPS (the lock icon in the top left of any browser connection) and is forced to be so by every modern browser, the primary means to track this info is to log what dns queries you are asking about, which in turn associates the IP address you're visiting. If you're using, say, T-Mobile on your phone, and have the default DNS server provided by T-Mobile, then T-Mobile has both pieces of information to see exactly what sites you're visiting. You ask T-Mobile what IP is associated to "mitchell.djunaedi.dev", they say "45.77.152.101" then you make a HTTPS connection to "45.77.152.101", it's trivial to tell what website you visited.

* Worse yet, if you connect to public wifi and use their DNS server, which would be the default on most devices, they can lie and serve you an incorrect IP address that they control, and when you think you're connecting to mitchell.djunaedi.dev hosted at 45.77.152.101, you're actually connecting to another IP on a webserver they control. Imagine if that were americanexpress.com or wellsfargo.com. Not good.

The solution? Configure a custom, trustworthy, no-logs, third-party DNS server to resolve all of your DNS queries. Often these are faster too, so the slight delay incurred by asking T-Mobile what the IP address is of a given domain will be reduced and your web browsing gets snappier.

* The DNS protocol in it's base form uses unencrypted traffic for sending this sensitive information, leading it susceptible to logging or hijacking. So while you're using another server for DNS info, your ISP can still snoop on this info fairly easily, but not as easy as they could if you were using their server. In fact, DNS servers must log queries, it's how they work, you'd have to modify their functionality and reduce their performance to make them no-log.
* Modern DNS protocols use encrypted connections, the most modern of which use the same HTTPS protocol that all other web traffic uses, so your queries remain unmodifiable and confidential and your ISP couldn't block you from using your own server because they'd likely block all web traffic in the process.

I recommend Quad9, it's a Swiss non-profit that has a strict no-logs policy and is, in my opinion, trustworthy with their no-logs claim. They're also very reliable and extremely fast, much faster than ISP provided DNS in my experience, especially the ECS enabled Quad9 server, more on that in a bit. If you configure your devices to resolve their queries with them, you are taking a huge leap in privacy, security and speed for free and like 2 minutes of your time for each device.

* For iOS go [here](https://docs.quad9.net/Setup_Guides/iOS/iOS_14_and_later_(Encrypted)/) and follow the documentation. That page also has other devices on the left. Apple is really annoying for making you have to download a settings configuration file, it shouldn't be that complicated, goes to show how much they really focus on "Privacy" huh? On Android and forks of it like GrapheneOS, it's built into the phone, you just add the domain of the DNS server and it just works. I'd only recommend choosing between 9.9.9.9 (DNSSEC, Threat-Blocking) or 9.9.9.11 (DNSSEC, Threat-Blocking, with ECS). Don't consider the others, they're mainly for debugging purposes. Also always choose HTTPS unless you have a reason to choose plain TLS (unlikely).
* [ECS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDNS_Client_Subnet) stands for EDNS Client Subnet. It basically allows Quad9 to provide the DNS nameservers it asks for DNS info with your general location. Most big websites use what are called [CDNs](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/cdn/what-is-a-cdn/) which are basically a bunch of different IP addresses for servers all across the world all associated with a single domain like google.com. If your domain server isn't provided with your approximate location, then they'll likely assign a server that is far away from you which would incur much more lag as you initiate the connection and your connection then gets rerouted to the closest server. I don't really think it's much of privacy leak since the site you connect to gets the very same approximate location that's associated with your IP when you connect, you're basically reducing speed for no gain. Due to this I use the 9.9.9.11 server with ECS enabled and recommend you do so also.

Do a before and after test. Go to [dnscheck.tools](https://dnscheck.tools/) and see what your IP address is, your ISP, then the IP address and ISP of your DNS resolver. If you haven't configured anything yet, you'll likely see your ISP provided DNS servers. That's bad basically universally. You either want to see your own IP (meaning you're running your own) or the IP of a server you've configured.

**TL;DR** your ISP sees whatever site you go to by default and logs it unless you configure your device to use an encrypted, no-logs privacy DNS provider like Quad9. Go [here](https://docs.quad9.net/Setup_Guides/iOS/iOS_14_and_later_(Encrypted)/) to configure your iPhone to use their HTTPS DNS server. Use the 9.9.9.11 server with ECS for extra speed. You'll also increase your security and speed of your internet beyond just privacy.

## Password Security

Password security is simple: use a strong password; either 6 random words or more or 20 characters with varying cases, numbers and special characters, and use a unique password for every account. While that's simple it's also impossible because you likely have 100+ accounts.

* Beyond this, always enable 2 Factor Authentication (2FA) when available. The order of their security is as follows, from least to most secure: SMS (Text Messages) one time code, email one time code, TOTP (Timed One Time Password, commonly called "Google Authenticator" though it's actually an open standard and can be used by mostly any 2FA app including password managers), FIDO U2F/Webauthn (Also called "Passkeys" or "Security Keys" these are the strongest form of 2FA/password and should always be used when available).

The solution to the multiple password manager. These applications will store all your passwords encrypted with one master password that you have to remember. They can autofill on websites to make it super convenient to log in, as well as being more secure. 

iOS comes with the Passwords app pre-installed as the default password manager. Going back to the previous points made about Free and Open Source Software, I highly recommend using a FOSS password manager like [Bitwarden](https://bitwarden.com/) or [ProtonPass](https://proton.me/pass), these integrate the same way as the Passwords app making them a convenient replacement for autofilling passwords.

* Using a FOSS password manager also helps reduce Apple's vendor lock in. Both Bitwarden and ProtonPass work via the browser, Linux, Android, Windows as well as iOS, Mac and iPadOS. If you stick to Apple's proprietary password manager, not only do you have to trust Apple's word that your passwords are secure, you also can only access them on Apple's platforms.

I'd recommend going through your accounts, starting with the most valuable (Google, Apple, Microsoft, etc. then your banking accounts), and changing your passwords and adding them to your new password manager. If you already use the Passwords app, you can easily export them to your new password manager.

**TL;DR** use strong passwords that are unique for each account. Enable 2FA when available choosing the strongest option available. Use a FOSS password manager like Bitwarden or ProtonPass to increase security and privacy and to reduce vendor lock-in.

## Messaging

Text messages are likely the most personal and revealing our personal data can get, safeguarding these communications are an utmost priority in my opinion. As I went over earlier in the Advanced Data Protection section, iMessage claims to be end-to-end encrypted and, with Advanced Data Protection, your iMessage backups are also end-to-end encrypted provided of course there are no [backdoors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRISM) or loopholes Apple is using in their legal documentation. HUGE problem here though. Almost everyone you text doesn't have Advanced Data Protection turned on with their key safely stored away such that a copy of every message you send is likely copied in another's iCloud account subject to warrantless surveillance.

* Another issue here is the SMS fallback. If you try to text someone and for whatever reason iMessage can't connect to Apple's servers, it will automatically send the message in the clear via the basic SMS texting protocol. You may have noticed this happen when your blue texts randomly get sent as a green text. Green messages are either SMS, MMS or RCS (the non iMessage standard texting protocols) and are unencrypted.

To solve both the trust issue with the proprietary iMessage code, iCloud backup issue, Apple walled garden vendor lock-in (iMessage only works between Apple devices), and SMS fallback we must use a more secure and private messenger. The largest one is [Signal](https://signal.org/). Signal is a non-profit that runs the Signal servers for the Signal app. Signal is an fully [open source](https://github.com/signalapp) end-to-end encrypted messenger is famous for it's cutting edge cryptography over the years. It has a large user base now, and is commonly found in-use in the government and the military. All you need to do is [download the app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/signal-private-messenger/id874139669), input your phone number to create an account, make a PIN, then allow Signal to access your contacts, then anyone in your contacts who has Signal will automatically show up.

* Feel a little apprehensive when I mentioned inputting your phone number and sharing your contacts? That's a good intuition, and is my major criticism of Signal. Unfortunately, with the convenience that comes with the phone number and contact model comes a major compromise on metadata. While Signal can't see the content of your messages, they can see who you're texting and when. This is way better than the iMessage scenario, and Signal claims in its [privacy policy](https://signal.org/legal/) that it keeps this who's texting who metadata to itself with no _Third Party_ sharing BUT privacy policies often change and it's something to keep in mind.

The best messenger in my opinion is [SimpleX Chat](https://simplex.chat/). It uses the same double ratchet end-to-end encryption algorithm as Signal but goes much further to protect the who-talks-to-who metadata. Unlike Signal, there are many SimpleX servers that act as one directional chat relays. These servers are fully FOSS, unlike Signal and can be hosted by anyone, also unlike Signal. There are no accounts in SimpleX, not even those based on a random username, let alone a phone number tied to your identity. Every chat connection must be shared as either a link or a QR code scanned. Every message sent is sent to a relay then sent to the recipient. The message sent from the recipient is sent to a different relay then to you, so there's no way to know who's talking to who unless the relays are being monitored by the same entity and that same entity knows who both you and your recipient is based on just their IP address. This is a really hard thing to accomplish, and basically requires a $100k plus warranted targeted deanonymization attack from state intelligence just to see who's texting who. Because of the end-to-end encryption they can't even see what you're saying.

**TL;DR** iMessage has some pretty big backdoors that make it untrustworthy for communication. Be very wary saying anything controversial on iMessage as data brokers, corporations, local law enforcement and state intelligence all have access to who you're messaging and what you're saying without need for a warrant. Use [Signal](https://signal.org/) or better yet, [SimpleX](https://simplex.chat/) instead.

## Email

[Email is an ancient and inherently insecure protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_Mail_Transfer_Protocol). It was created like 40 years ago and is completely unencrypted with no cryptographic signing by default, meaning anyone in the physical network path between you and the recipient can read and modify your email. There are multiple layers of protection built on top of email but these are inherently band-aids. TLS encryption (same S as the encryption used in HTTPS) is used for sending and receiving email most of the time, and there are DNS records set that help to make sure nobody else could send an email from your domain, but neither of these stops the fact that emails are sent unencrypted, recieved unencrypted, and stored unencrypted. Gmail knows everything that ever touched your inbox and [scans and process every single email that comes and goes using their AI algorithms](https://www.forbes.com/sites/zakdoffman/2025/05/07/googles-gmail-upgrade-you-must-decide-if-this-goes-too-far/). According to Gmail's privacy policy they don't share your email data with Third Parties which is funny because they ARE the Third Party here. They are themselves a giant data broker who stores your emails in servers across the world. 

iCloud email is basically the same, same privacy claims and same shortcomings.

There are encryption layers on top of normal email that can make it end-to-end encrypted, only viewable by the sender and receiver. The two main ones are OpenPGP and S/MIME. 

* S/MIME uses the same trust system as HTTPS to ensure that the person who is emailing you is really who they say they are. If I were to send an email signed with S/MIME he only way that it couldn't be me is if someone hacked my phone physically and sent the email, or if the US government conspired to break national and international law to compel SSL.com (the Certificate Authority for my S/MIME certificate) to forge my certificate. Politically impossible. If both users know each others S/MIME cert from having sent emails back and forth, they can then encrypt email beyond just signing it so nobody else can see what's said.
* OpenPGP uses a decentralized trust system, you upload your public key to a keyserver like [keys.openpgp.org](https://keys.openpgp.org/), then others can attest to that key being yours, you can make copies of that key on other servers, you can send emails to people and have them trust your key, etc. until a ["Web of Trust"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_of_trust) where one can find your key from multiple sources to have trust that that key is almost certainly yours, and thus any email signed with it comes from you. This is a more decentralized model, compared to the legal one described earlier with S/MIME.
* S/MIME encryption is a legal requirement for HIPAA (medical privacy law in the US) compliant email to maintain doctor-patient-confidentiality. Similarly it's legally required for attorney-client-privilege and for emails in the government and military. Because of this it's generally supported by enterprise clients like Gmail workspace, Microsoft Outlook, Thunderbird and Apple Mail. OpenPGP is used more often in services like [Protonmail](https://proton.me/mail) where PGP encryption is applied automatically between protonmail users and other users like mitchell@djunaedi.net who have their PGP keys set up in their domain records via [Web Key Directory](https://wiki.gnupg.org/WKD), and is also the core trust model for most software distribution.

[Protonmail](https://proton.me/) is what I recommend, like I just mention they apply PGP automatically. They also have a [strong privacy policy](https://proton.me/mail/privacy-policy), only logging log-in IP addresses. Emails are encrypted with your account password and stored encrypted after being received. End-to-end encrypted emails sent between Protonmail users or other PGP users are always encrypted. They're also based in Switzerland so even with a US warrant the data is harder to access for US authorities. You can also use a custom domain so for about $10/year on [Porkbun](https://porkbun.com/) (a domain name registrar, basically a place that sells domains) basically security, privacy and sovereigntymaxxing as much as possible your email, which is the most ubiquitous form of diogital communication. It's pretty important to get right IMO.

**TL;DR** Google uses AI to scan all of your emails and they're never encrypted. Switch to [Protonmail](https://proton.me/mail) for opportunistic end-to-end encryption, and at the least a strong privacy policy and encrypted-at-rest storage of your emails. Use a custom domain too so you can have an email address that you can take to any service and is not completely susceptible to account closure like a protonmail.com or gmail.com email is.

## WiFi Routers

Put simply, your Internet Service Provider (ISP) is not actually your "WiFi Company" necessarily. Generally their demarcation point is somewhere outside or within the house where their coaxial copper or fiber optic internet line stops. To convert either this copper signal or fiber optic signal to ethernet, which routers use, you need either a [cable modem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_modem) or [ONT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_interface_device#Optical_network_terminals), respectively. From then a router is connected, which serves 3 purposes:

* Performs [NAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_address_translation) which converts your public IP address that websites and peers see, into a private IP address on a per device basis.
* Assigns IP addresses automatically when devices connect by running a [DHCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_Host_Configuration_Protocol) server.
* Provides LAN access, which is the Local Area Network, the network that is kept separate from public internet traffic called the WAN (Wide Area Network). It provides LAN access via ethernet ports, and often via a bundled in WiFi Access Point.

The crucial point here is that most often people use their ISP's provided WiFi router. This expands the scope of the ISP's data access all the way into your local internet traffic and WiFi radio signals. You rely on the ISP with 100% trust to [secure your private network from external threats](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1634652/at-least-700000-routers-given-to-customers-by-isps-are-vulnerable-to-hacking-3.html), not [spy on your LAN traffic](https://stateofsurveillance.org/articles/surveillance/wifi-router-surveillance-isp-tracking/) passing through your router which are largely unencrypted, spy on your movements in your home collected via disruptions in WiFi signal [(100% true btw it's advertised as a feature)](https://www.xfinity.com/hub/smart-home/wifi-motion), and force you to use their DNS servers allowing them to get a list of every site visited and when (refer to the DNS section for details).

Not only all of this but they often are unstable and with poor WiFi range leading many to blame their whole internet service for being slow when it's likely just the cheap ISP spyware router.

The solution is to use your own router, ideally one that is 100% auditable via running Free and Open Source Firmware. The major WiFi router firmware project is [OpenWRT](https://openwrt.org/) which is the project that almost all WiFi routers and access points are based off of from all the big brands like TP-Link, Netgear and Ubiquiti, and almost certainly most ISP routers as well. OpenWRT is fully FOSS with a gaurantee of no spyware and with the ability to customize DNS servers, run its own DNS server, run an adblocking server, run a network wide VPN connection and more.

Most routers can be flashed with OpenWRT which can be found on their [Table of Hardware](https://toh.openwrt.org/), but this process is sometimes risky and difficult and voids the warranty. I'd recommend the official [OpenWRT One](https://www.amazon.com/youyeetoo-Banana-Pi-OpenWrt-One/dp/B0DJS7STYL/) Router which comes with OpenWRT pre-installed and is priced competitevly and equiped with lots of RAM and processing power to ensure that you can have many clients all pulling speeds way higher than your internet service provides.

**TL;DR** Your ISP at its core only provides your internet, you can opt into them providing a WiFi router as well, and you often have to pay monthly for this router. This router allows them to spy on you in very invasive ways, is often slow with short range, and is often insecure from external threats. I strongly advise switching to a FOSS router like the OpenWRT One for faster speeds, cutting edge security, and 100% privacy.
